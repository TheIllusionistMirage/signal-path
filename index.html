Ch 00 sampling · nyquist · quantisation · decibels · pitch

Sound as Numbers

Before any synthesis, one conversion: a physical pressure wave becomes a list of numbers. Every limitation you will fight later is already implied by that step.

What a sample actually is

Sound is a travelling variation in air pressure. A microphone converts it to a voltage; a converter measures that voltage at fixed intervals and writes down each measurement. That written-down number is a sample. It is not a tiny piece of sound, and it has no duration. It is the value of a continuous function at one instant.

Inside a synthesizer we skip the microphone. There is nothing to measure — we invent the numbers directly. The convention is a 32-bit float nominally in the range [-1.0, 1.0], where 0 is silence and ±1 is full scale. Floats rather than integers, because intermediate stages of a signal chain routinely exceed full scale and you want them to survive intact and come back rather than wrap around; the conversion to fixed-point integers happens once, at the very end, in the audio driver.

The sinusoid

One waveform matters more than all the others, because Chapter 2 will show that every other waveform is built from it:

the only equation you must memorise x(t) = A · sin(2π f t + φ)

A is amplitude and sets loudness. f is frequency in cycles per second and sets pitch. φ is the starting phase in radians. Change φ on a lone sine and you hear nothing at all — but φ is the difference between two oscillators reinforcing and cancelling, so it is quietly responsible for most of what makes a synthesizer sound thick. The module at the top of the page is exactly this equation, evaluated 48,000 times a second.

Sampling and the one hard limit

We take samples at a fixed sample rate fs, typically 44,100 or 48,000 per second. Between samples we know nothing. The Nyquist–Shannon theorem says how much that costs: a signal can be reconstructed perfectly from its samples if and only if it contains no energy at or above fs/2, a frequency called Nyquist.

Feed in something above Nyquist and it does not disappear and it does not distort gracefully. It folds: it comes out as a different, lower frequency, with no way to tell it apart from a tone that was genuinely there. A 30 kHz tone sampled at 48 kHz emerges as 48000 − 30000 = 18 kHz. This is aliasing, and it is the central antagonist of Chapter 3.

Bit depth and the noise floor

Sample rate quantises time. Bit depth quantises amplitude. With b bits you have 2b possible values, and the rounding error behaves like a small amount of added noise. The rule of thumb is about 6 dB of dynamic range per bit: 16-bit audio gives roughly 96 dB, 24-bit roughly 144 dB.

Working in f32 internally means about 24 bits of mantissa and an enormous exponent range, so quantisation stops being something you think about until output. Which is the point.

Decibels

Perceived loudness is roughly logarithmic, so amplitudes are quoted in decibels relative to full scale:

amplitude ratio to decibels dB = 20 · log₁₀(A) ⟺ A = 10^(dB/20)

Halving amplitude is −6 dB. A ten-fold reduction is −20 dB. Two identical signals summed give +6 dB; two uncorrelated signals of equal level give about +3 dB. That difference explains why stacking eight detuned oscillators is not eight times as loud, and why you must budget headroom when a polyphonic synth sums voices.

Pitch

Pitch is logarithmic too. An octave is a doubling of frequency, and twelve-tone equal temperament divides it into twelve equal ratios of 21/12 ≈ 1.05946. Anchored at MIDI note 69 = A440:

MIDI note number to frequency f(n) = 440 · 2^((n − 69) / 12)

One consequence worth internalising early: everything pitch-related should be done in the exponent. Detune is measured in cents (hundredths of a semitone, a factor of 21/1200). Filter cutoff modulation is measured in octaves, not hertz. Vibrato depth is a ratio, not an offset. Get this right at the start and every modulation you add later behaves the same way across the keyboard.

Rust · the whole of Chapter 0

// Cargo.toml: hound = "3"
const FS: f32 = 48_000.0;

fn midi_to_freq(n: f32) -> f32 { 440.0 * f32::powf(2.0, (n - 69.0) / 12.0) }

fn main() -> Result<(), Box<dyn std::error::Error>> {
    let spec = hound::WavSpec {
        channels: 1, sample_rate: FS as u32,
        bits_per_sample: 32, sample_format: hound::SampleFormat::Float,
    };
    let mut w = hound::WavWriter::create("tone.wav", spec)?;

    let freq = midi_to_freq(69.0);      // A440
    let mut phase: f32 = 0.0;        // normalised, [0,1)
    let inc = freq / FS;             // phase advance per sample

    for _ in 0..(FS as usize * 2) {
        let y = (std::f32::consts::TAU * phase).sin() * 0.3;
        w.write_sample(y)?;
        phase += inc;
        if phase >= 1.0 { phase -= 1.0; }
    }
    w.finalize()?;
    Ok(())
}
Why the phase variable

It would be simpler to write sin(TAU * freq * t) with a growing t. Do not. After a few minutes t is large enough that f32 can no longer resolve one sample step, and the tone develops a rasp. Worse, changing freq makes the argument jump, which is an audible click. Chapter 1 makes that click on purpose.

Ch 01 the audio callback · phase accumulators · real-time discipline

The Callback

A synthesizer is not a program that produces sound. It is a function that the sound card calls, on a deadline it does not negotiate.

The contract

The audio device runs on its own high-priority thread. Every so often it hands you a buffer and says: fill this, now. A block of 128 samples at 48 kHz is 2.67 ms of audio, which is also your entire compute budget. Miss it and you do not get a slow frame like in graphics — you get a click, because the driver plays whatever was in the buffer, which is silence or stale data.

Block sizeAt 48 kHzFeels like
641.33 msPlayable on a controller; unforgiving of any hitch
1282.67 msThe usual default. Good target.
51210.7 msNoticeable lag when playing; fine for playback
204842.7 msUnplayable, but very hard to underrun

Note that latency is at least two blocks, not one: you are filling block n + 1 while block n plays. Add the driver's own buffering and the round trip is typically three to five blocks.

The phase accumulator

The core primitive of every oscillator on this page is four lines long. Keep a phase in [0, 1). Add an increment each sample. Wrap. Map the phase to a waveform.

phase increment Δφ = f / fs φ ← (φ + Δφ) mod 1

Normalising phase to [0,1) rather than [0, 2π) is not cosmetic. The wrap becomes a subtraction of exactly 1.0, which is exact in floating point; wrapping at 2π accumulates error. It also makes Δφ directly meaningful: it is the fraction of a cycle traversed per sample, and it is the parameter PolyBLEP needs in Chapter 3.

The rules inside the callback

The audio thread must never block. Not for a lock, not for a page fault, not for the allocator, not for a log line. If it blocks, the deadline passes and you hear it.

  • No heap allocation. Preallocate every buffer, voice, and delay line before the stream starts. In Rust this means no Vec::push, no Box::new, no String, no collecting iterators.
  • No locks. A Mutex held by the UI thread during a GC pause or a scheduler preemption will stall the audio thread. Use lock-free single-producer queues (ringbuf) or atomics for scalar parameters.
  • No I/O, no syscalls, no println!.
  • No unbounded work. Worst case matters, not average. Eight voices must all fit in the budget even when every one of them is sounding.
  • Watch for denormals. Filter and reverb tails decay toward zero and enter subnormal float territory, where some CPUs take a large penalty per operation. Enable flush-to-zero, or add a tiny DC offset to the signal.

The demos on this page obey these rules. Each one runs in an AudioWorkletProcessor, which is the browser's audio thread; parameters arrive as messages and are copied into plain fields, and the analysis frames sent back to the page are written into a buffer allocated once at construction.

Rust · a real-time oscillator with cpal

// cpal = "0.15", ringbuf = "0.3"
struct Osc { phase: f32, inc: f32 }

impl Osc {
    fn set_freq(&mut self, f: f32, fs: f32) { self.inc = f / fs; }
    fn next(&mut self) -> f32 {
        let p = self.phase;
        self.phase += self.inc;
        if self.phase >= 1.0 { self.phase -= 1.0; }
        (std::f32::consts::TAU * p).sin()
    }
}

let device = cpal::default_host().default_output_device().unwrap();
let config = device.default_output_config()?.config();
let fs = config.sample_rate.0 as f32;
let ch = config.channels as usize;

let mut osc = Osc { phase: 0.0, inc: 0.0 };
osc.set_freq(220.0, fs);

let stream = device.build_output_stream(
    &config,
    move |out: &mut [f32], _| {
        // This closure IS the audio thread. Allocate nothing here.
        for frame in out.chunks_mut(ch) {
            let y = osc.next() * 0.25;
            for s in frame.iter_mut() { *s = y; }
        }
    },
    |err| eprintln!("stream error: {err}"),
    None,
)?;
stream.play()?;
Parameter changes are steps

Sending a new gain from the UI and assigning it directly means the signal jumps between two blocks. That jump is a click, exactly like the phase reset above. Chapter 7 fixes it properly with a one-pole smoother; for now, know that any parameter multiplying the signal needs slewing, and any parameter that only sets a frequency usually does not.

Ch 02 harmonic series · fourier · the DFT · windowing

Fourier

Timbre is spectrum. Once you can see a sound as a set of partials, most of synthesis becomes a question of which partials to make and which to remove.

The claim

Any periodic signal, however jagged, is a sum of sinusoids at integer multiples of its fundamental frequency. Those multiples are its harmonics or partials. The physical reason is that a bounded vibrating system — a string, an air column — only supports standing waves whose ends fit the boundary, and those fit at integer divisions of the length.

The three canonical synthesizer waveforms have famously simple recipes:

WaveHarmonics presentAmplitude of harmonic nCharacter
Sawtoothall1/nBrightest. Brass, strings.
Squareodd only1/nHollow, woody. Clarinet-ish.
Triangleodd only1/n²Nearly a sine. Soft flutes.
Sinethe first, alone—No timbre to speak of.

The 1/n² rolloff of the triangle is 12 dB per octave of harmonic number against the sawtooth's 6, which is why a triangle sounds so much darker even though both contain infinitely many partials.

From series to transform

The Fourier series describes periodic signals. Real sounds are not periodic forever, so we chop them into short frames and analyse each one with the Discrete Fourier Transform. The DFT correlates the frame against a complex exponential at every candidate frequency:

discrete fourier transform X[k] = Σn=0..N−1 x[n] · e−j2πkn/N

Euler's formula, e^(jθ) = cos θ + j sin θ, is why a single complex multiply captures both the in-phase and quadrature components at once — which is to say, both how much of that frequency is present and at what phase. The magnitude |X[k]| is what we plot; arg(X[k]) is the phase we usually discard.

The FFT is the same transform computed in O(N log N) instead of O(N²) by exploiting the symmetry of those exponentials. Three practical consequences:

  • Bin spacing is fs/N. At 48 kHz with N = 2048, each bin is 23.4 Hz wide. You cannot resolve two partials closer than that; you need a longer frame, which means worse time resolution. That trade is not a limitation of the algorithm, it is a property of the universe.
  • Only the first N/2 bins are meaningful. The rest are the conjugate mirror — the same Nyquist limit, seen from the other side.
  • Window your frame. Chopping a signal abruptly is multiplying by a rectangle, which convolves the spectrum with a very leaky kernel; a single pure sine smears across dozens of bins. A Hann window fixes most of it. Every spectrum on this page is Hann-windowed.

Additive synthesis, and why nobody uses it alone

You now have a complete synthesis method. Want a sawtooth? Sum sines at 1/n up to Nyquist. It is exact, it never aliases, and it is the ground truth against which Chapter 3's cheaper methods are judged.

It is also brutally expensive. A 55 Hz sawtooth at 48 kHz needs 436 partials — 436 sine evaluations per sample, per voice. Eight voices is 3,500 sines every 20 microseconds. That is why the rest of this book is, in a sense, a catalogue of ways to fake it.

Rust · analysing your own output

// rustfft = "6"
use rustfft::{FftPlanner, num_complex::Complex};

fn spectrum_db(x: &[f32], fs: f32) -> Vec<(f32, f32)> {
    let n = x.len();
    let mut buf: Vec<Complex<f32>> = x.iter().enumerate()
        .map(|(i, &s)| {
            // Hann window — without this a pure sine leaks everywhere
            let w = 0.5 - 0.5 * (std::f32::consts::TAU * i as f32 / (n as f32 - 1.0)).cos();
            Complex::new(s * w, 0.0)
        }).collect();

    FftPlanner::new().plan_fft_forward(n).process(&mut buf);

    buf.iter().take(n / 2).enumerate().map(|(k, c)| {
        let mag = c.norm() / (n as f32 / 4.0);
        (k as f32 * fs / n as f32, 20.0 * mag.max(1e-9).log10())
    }).collect()
}

Ch 03 aliasing · polyblep · wavetables · interpolation

Band-Limiting

The most obvious way to make a sawtooth is also the wrong one. This is the chapter where naive code starts to sound like broken code, and where most self-taught synth builders quietly give up.

Why the obvious thing fails

A sawtooth is a ramp from −1 to 1 that resets instantly. You already have a phase in [0,1), so y = 2·phase − 1 and you are done. Play it at 55 Hz and it sounds fine. Play it at 2 kHz and it sounds like a mistake.

The reason is the reset. An instantaneous jump has energy at every frequency — that is what Fig 2.2 was showing. An ideal sawtooth contains harmonics forever, and at 2 kHz only eleven of them fit below Nyquist. The rest do not vanish. They fold, per Fig 0.2, landing at frequencies that bear no harmonic relationship to the note you played.

That last part is what makes it so ugly. Distortion that adds harmonics sounds like distortion. Aliasing adds inharmonic partials, and worse, they move the wrong way: play a chromatic run upward and the aliased components descend, producing a shimmering metallic counter-melody that follows you around. Once you have heard it you will never mistake it for anything else.

PolyBLEP

We cannot remove aliasing after it happens; the folded partials are indistinguishable from real ones. We have to avoid generating them. The observation behind BLEP — band-limited step — is that a naive waveform differs from its band-limited ideal only in a tiny neighbourhood of each discontinuity. So compute the naive waveform, then add a correction shaped like the difference.

A properly band-limited step is the integral of a sinc, which is expensive and infinite in extent. PolyBLEP replaces it with a two-piece quadratic covering just one sample either side of the jump. It is not exact — it typically knocks 40–60 dB off the aliasing rather than eliminating it — but it costs a handful of arithmetic operations, which is why nearly every virtual-analogue oscillator shipping today uses it.

Rust · the whole algorithm

/// t  = phase distance from the discontinuity, in [0,1)
/// dt = phase increment per sample = freq / sample_rate
fn poly_blep(mut t: f32, dt: f32) -> f32 {
    if t < dt {
        t /= dt;
        t + t - t * t - 1.0            // just after the jump
    } else if t > 1.0 - dt {
        t = (t - 1.0) / dt;
        t * t + t + t + 1.0            // just before the jump
    } else {
        0.0                            // nowhere near a jump: leave it alone
    }
}

fn saw(&mut self) -> f32 {
    let (p, dt) = (self.phase, self.inc);
    self.advance();
    (2.0 * p - 1.0) - poly_blep(p, dt)
}

fn square(&mut self, pw: f32) -> f32 {
    let (p, dt) = (self.phase, self.inc);
    self.advance();
    let mut y = if p < pw { 1.0 } else { -1.0 };
    y += poly_blep(p, dt);                       // rising edge at phase 0
    y -= poly_blep((p - pw + 1.0) % 1.0, dt);    // falling edge at phase pw
    y
}

A square has two discontinuities per cycle, so it needs two corrections with opposite signs. A triangle has no discontinuity in value at all — only in slope — so it needs a different correction (PolyBLAMP), or, more cheaply, you integrate a band-limited square with a leaky one-pole. That is what this book's triangle does.

Wavetables

The other mainstream answer trades memory for arithmetic. Precompute one cycle of the waveform into a table by additive synthesis — expensive, but done once at startup — and then just read the table back at the right rate.

The catch is that a table built with 400 harmonics is only safe for low notes. So you build one table per octave, each containing only the harmonics that still fit under Nyquist at the top of that octave, and select the appropriate table by pitch. Eleven tables of 2048 floats is 88 KB — nothing — and playback is one lookup and one interpolation regardless of how rich the waveform is.

Interpolation is not free either

Linear interpolation between table entries is itself a lowpass filter with a small amount of distortion — the error shows up as a noise floor around −60 to −70 dB with a 2048-point table. Cubic (Catmull-Rom) interpolation buys you roughly another 20 dB for about three times the arithmetic. The same choice reappears in Chapter 8, where a chorus reads a delay line at a fractional position.

Two things PolyBLEP gives you for free

Pulse-width modulation. Move the falling edge of a square away from the halfway point and the spectrum changes dramatically — at 50% the even harmonics cancel completely, and as the width narrows they return. Sweep it slowly with an LFO and you get the thick, breathing texture that defines a great deal of 1980s synth pop. Because PolyBLEP corrects each edge independently, this costs nothing extra.

Hard sync. Reset oscillator B's phase every time oscillator A completes a cycle. B's pitch is then locked to A, but its timbre is set by B's own frequency, which you can sweep freely. The forced reset is a discontinuity, so it needs its own BLEP correction — and it produces the tearing, aggressive sound that made the sync lead a genre unto itself.

Ch 04 ADSR · exponential curves · gates · velocity · MIDI

Envelopes

A continuous tone is not a note. What turns one into the other is a shape in time — and the ear cares more about the first 50 milliseconds of that shape than about almost anything else in the signal.

Why attack transients carry identity

Record a piano note and a trumpet note at the same pitch and loudness, then splice off the first tenth of a second of each. Listeners struggle to tell them apart. The steady-state spectra of many instruments are surprisingly similar; the attack — how fast, in what order the partials arrive, with what noise — is where the instrument lives. This is why a synth patch with a lazy envelope sounds generic no matter how good the oscillator.

ADSR

The standard shape has four parameters and, importantly, is driven by a gate: a boolean that is true while the key is held.

  • Attack — time from 0 to full, starting at note-on.
  • Decay — time from full down to the sustain level.
  • Sustain — a level, not a time. Held for as long as the gate is true.
  • Release — time from wherever the envelope is when the gate falls, down to zero.

The asymmetry catches everyone once: three times and one level. It follows from the gate being the thing in charge. Sustain has no duration because its duration is the player's business.

Why the curve has to be exponential

Physical systems lose energy in proportion to how much energy they have, which produces exponential decay. Our perception of loudness is also roughly logarithmic. A linear ramp down to zero therefore sounds like it hangs at a constant loudness and then suddenly disappears at the end; an exponential decay sounds like a decay.

The cheap and idiomatic way to get one is a one-pole filter aimed at a target:

one-pole envelope segment v ← target + (v − target) · c c = exp(−1 / (τ · fs))

For decay and release, target is the destination. For attack there is a trick: aim at something above 1.0 — say 1.25 — and stop when you cross 1.0. Aim exactly at 1.0 and you never arrive, because an exponential approaches asymptotically. The overshoot target also gives the attack a pleasantly convex shape rather than a decelerating one.

Velocity, and what to do with it

MIDI note-on carries a velocity from 1 to 127. Mapping it straight to amplitude is the obvious move and the least convincing one, because on a real instrument playing harder does not merely make the sound louder — it makes it brighter, because more energy goes into the higher modes. So route velocity to amplitude and to filter cutoff, which is exactly what the synth in Chapter 7 does.

Two details worth getting right. First, map velocity through a curve rather than linearly; (v/127)² or similar feels far more natural on most controllers. Second, a note-on with velocity 0 is defined by the MIDI spec to mean note-off, and a surprising amount of hardware relies on that. Handle it or you will hang notes.

Rust · MIDI in, without touching the audio thread

// midir = "0.9", ringbuf = "0.3"
enum Ev { On { note: u8, vel: u8 }, Off { note: u8 }, Cc { num: u8, val: u8 } }

let rb = ringbuf::HeapRb::<Ev>::new(256);
let (mut tx, mut rx) = rb.split();

// --- MIDI thread: parse and push. Never touches the synth. ---
let _conn = midi_in.connect(&port, "in", move |_stamp, msg, _| {
    if msg.len() < 3 { return; }
    let ev = match msg[0] & 0xF0 {
        0x90 if msg[2] > 0 => Ev::On { note: msg[1], vel: msg[2] },
        0x90 | 0x80        => Ev::Off { note: msg[1] },   // vel 0 == note off
        0xB0               => Ev::Cc { num: msg[1], val: msg[2] },
        _ => return,
    };
    let _ = tx.push(ev);          // full queue drops, never blocks
}, ())?;

// --- audio thread: drain, then render. No locks, no allocation. ---
move |out: &mut [f32], _| {
    while let Some(ev) = rx.pop() {
        match ev {
            Ev::On { note, vel } => synth.note_on(note, vel as f32 / 127.0),
            Ev::Off { note }     => synth.note_off(note),
            Ev::Cc { num, val }  => synth.control(num, val as f32 / 127.0),
        }
    }
    synth.render(out);
}
Sample-accurate timing, when you need it

Draining the whole queue at the top of the block quantises every event to the block boundary — up to 2.67 ms of jitter, which is audible on fast passages. The fix is to timestamp events with a sample offset within the block and split rendering at those offsets. Worth doing eventually. Not worth doing first.

Ch 05 difference equations · poles & zeros · zero-delay feedback · ladders

Filters

The longest chapter, and the one that repays study. Oscillators give a synthesizer its raw material; the filter is what makes it expressive.

Subtractive synthesis in one sentence

Start with a harmonically rich waveform, remove parts of its spectrum, and modulate what you remove over time. That is the entire architecture of the Minimoog, the Prophet-5, the TB-303, and most software synths since.

The difference equation

A digital filter computes each output sample from recent inputs and recent outputs:

the general form y[n] = b₀x[n] + b₁x[n−1] + b₂x[n−2] − a₁y[n−1] − a₂y[n−2]

The b terms are feedforward and create zeros: frequencies that get cancelled. The a terms are feedback and create poles: frequencies that get emphasised. A filter with only b terms is FIR — finite impulse response, unconditionally stable, and it takes many taps to be selective. Add feedback and it becomes IIR, which is dramatically more efficient and can also explode.

Poles, zeros, and the unit circle

Apply the z-transform (substituting z⁻¹ for "delayed by one sample") to the difference equation and you get a ratio of polynomials:

transfer function H(z) = (b₀ + b₁z⁻¹ + b₂z⁻²) / (1 + a₁z⁻¹ + a₂z⁻²)

Roots of the numerator are zeros; roots of the denominator are poles. Evaluate H around the unit circle z = e^(jω) and you have the frequency response — the magnitude at each frequency is the product of distances to the zeros divided by the product of distances to the poles. That geometric picture is worth internalising: a pole near the circle means a nearby frequency gets divided by something tiny, hence a resonant peak.

The one-pole

Start here, because you can derive it. An RC lowpass is a first-order differential equation; the simplest discretisation gives:

one-pole lowpass y[n] = y[n−1] + g · (x[n] − y[n−1]) g = 1 − e^(−2π fc / fs)

One multiply, two adds, one state variable. It rolls off at 6 dB/octave, has no resonance, and cannot go unstable for g in [0,1]. Subtract its output from its input and you have a highpass for free. You will use this constantly — for DC blocking, for damping in a reverb, for smoothing parameters, for the envelope in Chapter 4.

The biquad, and its limitation

Two poles and two zeros gets you 12 dB/octave and genuine resonance. The RBJ cookbook gives closed forms for lowpass, highpass, bandpass, notch, shelves and peaks in terms of cutoff and Q, and they are exact — a biquad matched to an analogue prototype is indistinguishable from it, at a fixed cutoff.

The trouble is modulation. Those coefficients are a nonlinear function of cutoff, and the filter's two state variables carry a memory that was consistent with the old coefficients. Recompute them every sample while an envelope sweeps and the state no longer matches; you get zipper noise at best and loud transients at worst. Recompute them once per block and you get stepping. Biquads are superb for EQ and poor for the thing a synth filter is for.

Zero-delay feedback, and why it exists

An analogue state-variable filter is a loop of two integrators with feedback. Translating it naively means the feedback needs the output before the output has been computed — a delay-free loop, which code cannot express. The old solution was to insert a one-sample delay in the loop. It works, but that delay is a phase error that grows with frequency, so as you raise cutoff the resonant peak drifts flat and the filter eventually blows up.

The modern solution, developed properly in Vadim Zavalishin's The Art of VA Filter Design, is to solve the loop algebraically. Write the integrator in its trapezoidal (TPT) form, substitute, and the instantaneous feedback resolves into a set of coefficients you can precompute. The result is a filter that stays accurate and stable while cutoff is modulated at audio rate.

TPT SVF — one sample g = tan(π·fc/fs) k = 1/Q a₁ = 1/(1 + g(g+k)) a₂ = g·a₁ a₃ = g·a₂

Rust · the filter you should actually ship

pub struct Svf { g: f32, k: f32, a1: f32, a2: f32, a3: f32, ic1: f32, ic2: f32, fs: f32 }

impl Svf {
    pub fn set_cutoff(&mut self, fc: f32) {
        self.g = (std::f32::consts::PI * fc.clamp(12.0, self.fs * 0.47) / self.fs).tan();
        self.update();
    }
    pub fn set_res(&mut self, r: f32) {
        self.k = 2.0 - 2.0 * r.clamp(0.0, 0.985);   // k -> 0 is self-oscillation
        self.update();
    }
    fn update(&mut self) {
        self.a1 = 1.0 / (1.0 + self.g * (self.g + self.k));
        self.a2 = self.g * self.a1;
        self.a3 = self.g * self.a2;
    }
    /// Returns (lowpass, bandpass, highpass) — all three, same cost.
    pub fn process(&mut self, x: f32) -> (f32, f32, f32) {
        let v3 = x - self.ic2;
        let v1 = self.a1 * self.ic1 + self.a2 * v3;
        let v2 = self.ic2 + self.a2 * self.ic1 + self.a3 * v3;
        self.ic1 = 2.0 * v1 - self.ic1;
        self.ic2 = 2.0 * v2 - self.ic2;
        (v2, v1, x - self.k * v1 - v2)
    }
}

Notice that lowpass, bandpass and highpass all fall out of the same state — and that notch = high + low and peak = high − low. One filter, five responses, no extra arithmetic. That is why it is called a state-variable filter.

The ladder, and nonlinearity

Moog's transistor ladder is four one-pole stages in series with global negative feedback. Four poles means 24 dB/octave, and the feedback path sets resonance. In a purely linear implementation, pushing feedback past the critical point makes it self-oscillate into an ever-growing sine.

In the real circuit, the transistors saturate. That single nonlinearity does several things at once: it limits self-oscillation to a stable amplitude, it generates harmonics that thicken the resonant peak, and it makes the filter respond differently to loud and quiet inputs. Put a tanh in the feedback path of your ladder and you get most of it. Leave it out and you have a filter that is technically correct and sounds sterile.

Nonlinearity means aliasing again

Saturation generates harmonics, and harmonics above Nyquist fold — the same problem as Chapter 3, arriving from a new direction. The standard mitigation is oversampling: upsample 2× or 4×, run the nonlinear stage, filter, downsample. It is the main reason a good software filter costs so much more than the arithmetic in its difference equation suggests.

Keyboard tracking

A fixed cutoff removes a different proportion of the harmonics from a low note than from a high one, so high notes sound progressively duller. The fix is to scale cutoff with pitch:

key tracking, amount in [0,1] fc_effective = fc · (f_note / f_ref) ^ amount

At amount 1.0 the filter tracks the keyboard exactly and timbre is constant across the range; at 0 it is fixed. Somewhere between 0.3 and 0.7 usually sounds most like an instrument. This is your first real taste of the principle that governs Chapter 6 — modulation belongs in the exponent.

Ch 06 LFOs · phase modulation · sidebands · routing

Modulation

Nothing in an interesting sound holds still. Modulation is the machinery for making things move — and when you speed it up past about 20 Hz it stops being movement and becomes timbre.

Low-frequency oscillators

An LFO is the same phase accumulator from Chapter 1 running below the hearing threshold. Its output is a control signal, and where you send it decides what it is called:

DestinationNameTypical depth
Oscillator pitchVibrato±10 to 50 cents, 4–7 Hz
AmplitudeTremolo±10–30%, 3–8 Hz
Filter cutoffWobble / auto-wah±0.5 to 3 octaves
Pulse widthPWM±30%, 0.2–2 Hz
Delay timeChorus / flanger±2–5 ms, 0.3–1 Hz

Two implementation notes that matter more than they look. Depth is exponential for anything pitch-like — a vibrato of "±20 Hz" is enormous on a bass note and inaudible on a high one, whereas "±20 cents" is the same everywhere. And free-running versus retriggered is a musical decision: a free-running LFO makes repeated notes differ from one another, which sounds alive; a retriggered one makes them identical, which sounds tight. Offer both.

Sample-and-hold deserves a mention: an LFO that latches a random value once per cycle. Route it to cutoff and you have the bubbling sound that defines a certain kind of sequenced electronic music.

FM is really PM

Push a modulator into the audio range and something different happens. Rather than hearing the pitch move, you hear a new, static timbre — because the modulation is now fast enough to create sidebands.

Here is the thing everyone gets wrong: what Yamaha sold as FM, and what almost every "FM" synth implements, is phase modulation. The modulator is added to the carrier's phase, not to its frequency:

phase modulation y(t) = sin(2π fc t + I · sin(2π fm t))

The two are mathematically related — frequency is the derivative of phase — but PM is far better behaved in practice. True FM integrates the modulator, so any DC offset in it makes the carrier's pitch drift, and stacking operators compounds the problem. PM has no such issue, which is why it won.

Sidebands

The spectrum of the equation above is a carrier plus pairs of sidebands at fc ± k·fm for every integer k. The amplitude of the k-th pair is the Bessel function of the first kind, Jk(I), where I is the modulation index.

You do not need to compute Bessel functions, and you should not try to memorise them. You need three facts from them:

  • Index controls brightness. At I = 0 only the carrier exists — a pure sine. Raising I pushes energy outward into higher-order sidebands. Roughly, significant sidebands extend to about k ≈ I + 1.
  • The carrier is not always the loudest component. J₀ passes through zero at I ≈ 2.4, so at that index the fundamental disappears entirely. This is a real effect you can hear, and it is why FM patches can sound like they change pitch when they do not.
  • Negative-frequency sidebands fold and phase-invert. When fc − k·fm goes below zero it reflects around DC, and if it lands on an existing partial it can cancel it. Not a bug — this is a large part of what makes FM spectra so distinctive and so hard to predict by ear.

The ratio decides harmonic or not

Sidebands sit at fc ± k·fm. If fm/fc is a simple integer ratio, every sideband lands exactly on a harmonic of the carrier, and the result is a pitched tone. If the ratio is irrational, the sidebands fall between the harmonics, the result is inharmonic, and your ear reports "bell", "gong", or "metal".

RatioResult
1 : 1Sawtooth-like, all harmonics. Brass and reeds.
2 : 1Odd harmonics only. Clarinet, hollow.
3 : 1, 5 : 1Sparse harmonic spectra. Woodwind-ish.
1.41 : 1Inharmonic. Bells, tubular metal.
3.14 : 1Deeply inharmonic. Gongs, sound design.

Add an envelope on the index and you have the FM electric piano: a bright inharmonic strike that settles into a nearly pure sine as the index falls. Two operators and two envelopes reproduce one of the most recognisable sounds of the 1980s.

Ring modulation, and routing

Multiplying two signals rather than adding gives ring modulation: sum and difference frequencies only, no carrier. With two sines you get exactly two partials at f₁ ± f₂ — the fastest route to inharmonic, clangorous, distinctly artificial tones. Amplitude modulation is the same operation with a DC offset on one input, which keeps the carrier in the output.

Once you have several sources and several destinations you need a modulation matrix: a list of (source, destination, depth) triples evaluated per sample or per block. Build this abstraction before you have five modulations, not after. Hard-coding "LFO1 goes to cutoff" into the voice is the single most common architectural mistake in a first synth, and unpicking it later touches every line of the render loop.

Ch 07 voices · stealing · smoothing · denormals · budgets

Voices

Everything so far made one sound at a time. Turning that into an instrument is mostly an exercise in resource management — which is the part of this book your systems instincts already cover.

The voice

A voice is one complete signal chain: oscillators, filter, envelopes, and the state they need. Polyphony is N voices summed. That is the whole idea, and it is worth stating plainly because the interesting problems are all about allocation rather than DSP.

Design decision to make early: every voice owns all its state. No shared filter, no shared envelope. It seems wasteful and it is not — the alternative is a tangle where one note's release affects another's tone. The only things reasonably shared are the global LFOs and the effects, which sit after the summing point.

Allocation and stealing

On note-on you need a voice. In order of preference:

  1. A voice already playing this exact note — retrigger it, so repeated notes do not stack up into a volume spike.
  2. Any completely idle voice.
  3. Failing both, steal one.

Stealing policy is audible. Taking the oldest voice is the standard choice and works well for keyboard playing. Taking the quietest — the one furthest into its release — is less noticeable but costs you a scan. Taking the lowest note is wrong for bass lines and right for pads. Whatever you pick, do not simply cut the stolen voice off: give it a very short forced release, a few milliseconds, before restarting. Otherwise you get a click on every stolen note, which is exactly when the texture is busiest and least able to hide it.

Smoothing

Chapter 1 warned about this; here is where it bites. Any parameter that multiplies the signal — gain, mix, effect depth — must be slewed, or every knob movement is a staircase of small steps, each one a discontinuity. The audible result is called zipper noise and it sounds exactly like the name.

one-pole parameter smoother v ← target + (v − target) · c c = exp(−1 / (τ · fs))

A time constant around 5–20 ms is right for gains. Parameters that only set a frequency — oscillator pitch, filter cutoff — often do not need it, because the filter or oscillator absorbs the step; but cutoff on a high-resonance filter is an exception, and glide (portamento) is just a deliberately slow smoother on pitch.

Denormals

A filter's tail decays exponentially toward zero and eventually reaches values around 10−38, where floats become subnormal. On some x86 CPUs every operation on a subnormal takes a microcoded slow path, and a reverb decaying into silence can cost more than the entire rest of your synth. Symptoms: CPU usage that spikes after you stop playing.

Two fixes. Set the CPU's flush-to-zero and denormals-are-zero flags once at stream startup, or add a tiny constant — around 10−20 — to the signal at a few points in the feedback paths. The first is cleaner; the second is portable.

Block processing

Per-sample code like everything in this book is the clearest way to learn. Once it works, the standard optimisation is to hoist everything that does not change per sample out of the inner loop: coefficient updates, envelope stage checks, parameter reads. Many synths update modulation once every 8, 16 or 32 samples — a "control rate" — and interpolate between updates. The saving is large, the audible cost is usually nil, and the risk is that a fast envelope gets quantised into steps. Keep amplitude envelopes at full rate.

Rust · the allocator

pub struct Synth { voices: [Voice; 8], counter: u64 }

impl Synth {
    pub fn note_on(&mut self, note: u8, vel: f32) {
        self.counter += 1;
        let stamp = self.counter;

        // 1. same note already sounding -> retrigger it
        if let Some(v) = self.voices.iter_mut()
            .find(|v| v.note == note && v.held) {
            return v.trigger(note, vel, stamp);
        }
        // 2. a free voice
        if let Some(v) = self.voices.iter_mut().find(|v| !v.active()) {
            return v.trigger(note, vel, stamp);
        }
        // 3. steal the oldest. min_by_key, not sort - no allocation.
        let v = self.voices.iter_mut()
            .min_by_key(|v| v.stamp).unwrap();
        v.steal(note, vel, stamp);   // 3 ms forced fade, then retrigger
    }

    pub fn render(&mut self, out: &mut [f32]) {
        out.fill(0.0);
        for v in self.voices.iter_mut().filter(|v| v.active()) {
            v.add_into(out);     // accumulate, never allocate a temp buffer
        }
    }
}
Measure the worst case

Time your callback with all voices sounding, maximum resonance, and every effect engaged — not the average. Divide by the block duration to get a load fraction. Anything above about 0.5 will glitch on a busy machine, because you are sharing the CPU with a browser, a DAW, and a scheduler that does not know your deadline exists.

Ch 08 delay lines · combs · reverb · saturation · plugins

Space & Shipping

One data structure — a circular buffer read at a fractional offset — generates chorus, flanging, delay and reverb. After that, the remaining work is packaging.

The delay line

Allocate a buffer, keep a write index, and read from write − delay, wrapping around. Because the delay is rarely a whole number of samples — and because modulating it is the entire point — the read is interpolated, exactly as in the wavetable of Chapter 3.

Combs, allpasses and Schroeder reverb

A delay with feedback is a comb filter: its response is a series of evenly spaced peaks at multiples of 1/delay, because those frequencies come back in phase every time round the loop. Short delays give widely spaced, audibly pitched combs; that hollow ringing is what a flanger is.

An allpass filter passes every frequency at the same level but delays them by different amounts. Musically it does almost nothing on its own, which is exactly why it is useful: it smears a transient into a dense cloud without colouring the spectrum.

Schroeder's 1962 reverb combines them: several parallel combs with mutually prime delay lengths to build echo density without the echoes lining up, followed by a chain of allpasses to diffuse the result. Add a one-pole lowpass inside each comb's feedback path and high frequencies decay faster than low ones — which is what real rooms do, and without it a reverb sounds like a metal tank.

Modern designs use feedback delay networks — a matrix that mixes several delay lines into each other — which give smoother decay for similar cost. Schroeder is where to start; the intuition transfers.

Chorus

Take a short delay, modulate its length with a slow LFO, and mix it back with the dry signal. Because the read pointer is moving, the delayed copy is Doppler-shifted — it detunes slightly against the original, and the beating between them is the effect. Two or three copies at different LFO rates give the lush version. This is also why chorus and vibrato are the same device with different mix settings.

Saturation

A waveshaper maps input amplitude to output amplitude through a nonlinear curve — tanh, a polynomial, a hard clip. Gentle curves add low-order harmonics and read as warmth or glue; hard ones add high-order harmonics and read as distortion.

And, once again: harmonics above Nyquist fold. A hard clipper on a bright signal aliases badly. Oversample the nonlinear stage — upsample 4×, shape, lowpass, decimate — or use a curve chosen to be band-limited. This is the last appearance of the problem introduced in Chapter 0, and by now it should feel inevitable.

One correction to Chapter 8

Chapter 8 recommends nih-plug for building CLAP and VST3 plugins. Since that text was written, the RustAudio community has forked it as nice-plug, a community-led project that now describes itself as the recommended toolkit for Rust audio plugin development. nih-plug remains a reasonable starting point and the API is closely related, but check the current state of both before committing.

How it was made

Every waveform, spectrum, filter response and envelope on this page was computed in the browser by the DSP source embedded in this file — the same source that is compiled into a Blob and loaded as the AudioWorklet you have been listening to. No OscillatorNode, no BiquadFilterNode, no pre-rendered audio and no pictures of plots. Nothing here is an illustration of a measurement; everything is the measurement.

One HTML file, no build step, no dependencies beyond three webfonts. View source: the DSP is in a <script type="text/plain"> block near the bottom, in the same form the text describes it.
